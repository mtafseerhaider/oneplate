# Lecture 9 — Source Verification Audit

*A Guide To Islamic Child Raising (Tarbiyah)*, Part 9 — Shaykh Usama Abdulghani (Light Of Guidance)
Transcript: `transcripts/lecture-09-transcript.txt` (YouTube auto-captions, 40:48) · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1MoAzcfMEzY
Audited against `research/_verified-index.md`; items already settled there are cross-referenced, not re-researched.

---

## Headline findings

- **This lecture cites no Qur'an at all and only three or four narrations.** Its weight rests almost entirely on empirical and developmental claims about screens, play and child health. It must therefore be judged mostly as a science talk, not a hadith talk — and that is how this audit weights its effort.
- **The "beware of two traits" narration is real and his gloss of it is exactly right — but he attributed it to the wrong Imam.** The version whose two consequence-clauses he reproduces ("the lazy will not fulfil a right; the vexed will not persevere on a right") is **Imam al-Baqir**, in *Tuhaf al-'Uqul*, short maxims no. 31 — chainless. The *al-Kafi* version that **is** from Imam al-Sadiq has a completely different second half and is graded ***da'if*** by al-Majlisi. He has, in effect, spliced al-Baqir's wording onto al-Sadiq's name.
- **The Hajj/Medina case study (Muhammad and "Murazim") could NOT be located** in any primary collection reachable in this session — not in *al-Kafi*'s chapters on visiting the sick (3/3/6, 3/3/8) nor its chapter on fulfilling a believer's need (2/1/83). **Do not attribute it to a source.** A structurally identical and genuinely attested narration exists (Aban b. Taghlib and the tawaf — *al-Kafi* 2, ch. 83, h. 6, *majhul*) and is the better citation.
- **The Allamah Tabataba'i story (the reward of all of *al-Mizan* for one night nursing a sick mother) is NOT LOCATED.** His two factual asides around it are close but not exact: *al-Mizan* was written **1954–1972, i.e. ~18 years**, not 20; the standard Arabic edition is **20 volumes**. Also worth knowing: **Allamah Tabataba'i lost his own mother at age five**, so the story cannot be read as autobiographical.
- **On the science he is a mixed but often better-than-average speaker.** Three of his claims are genuinely well supported (outdoor play → immune regulation; play/exercise → executive function and memory; adventurous play → lower anxiety risk). Two are overstated in a way that matters ("adrenaline rush greater than physical play, after which play no longer registers"; "eliminates anxiety"). One — **"nervous tics"** — is the single most surprising item in the lecture: it is *better* supported than he seems to know, but for a phenomenon (social-media-induced functional tic-like behaviour in adolescents) quite different from the toddler-and-video-games picture he paints.
- **"Non-renewable energy of the first seven years" is his own coinage, not a scientific or scriptural concept** — but it gestures at something real (motor-competence "proficiency barrier", sensitive periods). The **lazy-eye analogy is the best-chosen thing in the lecture**: amblyopia genuinely is a sensitive-period condition treated by patching the strong eye, and it is a fair model for "act early." One specific needs softening — treatment is not futile after childhood, only less effective.

## Narrations

### N1. Imam al-Sadiq — "Beware of two traits"

> *Transcript [21:27]:* "So he says this beware of two traits. Imam Sodak says … one of them being not just not having motivation, not having drive, not having push, and the other one is laziness. The Imam says this, 'If I'm a person who doesn't have motivation, I won't be able to see things through…' The other one he says, 'And if you're lazy, then actually you won't observe the rights that others have.'"

There are **two distinct narrations** here, and the speaker has merged them.

---

**(a) The version he actually glossed — Imam al-Baqir**

**Arabic**
> إِيَّاكَ وَالْكَسَلَ وَالضَّجَرَ، فَإِنَّهُمَا مِفْتَاحُ كُلِّ شَرٍّ؛ مَنْ كَسِلَ لَمْ يُؤَدِّ حَقّاً، وَمَنْ ضَجِرَ لَمْ يَصْبِرْ عَلَى حَقٍّ.

**Translation**
> "Beware of laziness (*al-kasal*) and vexation/despondency (*al-dajar*), for the two of them are the key to every evil: whoever is lazy does not fulfil a right, and whoever is vexed does not persevere upon a right."

**Reference** — Ibn Shu'ba al-Harrani, ***Tuhaf al-'Uqul***, "Short Maxims of Imam al-Baqir ('a)", **maxim no. 31**. (al-islam.org's English edition renders it: *"Beware of laziness and tedium. They are the keys to every evil. The lazy will not fulfill the rights (that are incumbent upon them) and the tedious will not submit to the right."*) The same maxim collection carries a companion at no. 63: *"Laziness injures both the worldly and the religious affairs."*

**Chain** — **None.** *Tuhaf al-'Uqul* omits all isnads by the compiler's own stated method.

**Authenticity** — **Ungradeable.** As the shared index establishes for *Tuhaf*, there is no chain to assess, so no rijal scholar has issued or can issue a verdict. It is a *mursal* maxim of a respected 4th-century compiler.

**Confidence** — **MODERATELY VERIFIED.** The text and its placement in *Tuhaf* are confirmed; the attribution rests on the compiler's authority alone.

**Verdict on the speaker's use** — **Content: excellent. Attribution: wrong Imam.**
He is *more* accurate than most speakers on the substance. The two consequences he gives map onto the Arabic precisely and in the right order:
- *dajar* (which he renders "not having motivation, not having drive, not having push") → *lam yasbir 'ala haqq*, "will not persevere upon a right" → his "I won't be able to see things through." ✔
- *kasal* (laziness) → *lam yu'addi haqqan*, "does not fulfil a right" → his "you won't observe the rights that others have." ✔

That pairing is easy to invert and he did not invert it. Credit where due. But he says "**Imam Sodak** says," and this wording is **Imam al-Baqir's**.

---

**(b) The version that IS from Imam al-Sadiq — and it says something else**

**Arabic**
> سَهْلُ بْنُ زِيَادٍ عَنِ ابْنِ مَحْبُوبٍ عَنْ سَعْدِ بْنِ أَبِي خَلَفٍ عَنْ أَبِي الْحَسَنِ مُوسَى (عليه السلام) قَالَ: قَالَ أَبِي (عليه السلام) لِبَعْضِ وُلْدِهِ: إِيَّاكَ وَالْكَسَلَ وَالضَّجَرَ، فَإِنَّهُمَا يَمْنَعَانِكَ مِنْ حَظِّكَ مِنَ الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ.

**Translation**
> Sa'd b. Abi Khalaf, from Abu'l-Hasan Musa [al-Kazim] ('a), who said: "My father ['a] said to one of his sons: 'Beware of laziness and vexation, for the two of them **deprive you of your portion of this world and the hereafter**.'"

**Reference** — ***al-Kafi***, vol. 5, *Kitab al-Ma'isha*, chapter "Detestability of Laziness" (*karahiyat al-kasal*), **h. 2** — thaqalayn.com/hadith/5/2/10/2 (standard Arabic ed. c. 5:85).

**Chain** — Sahl b. Ziyad ← Ibn Mahbub ← Sa'd b. Abi Khalaf ← Imam al-Kazim ← his father Imam al-Sadiq. Note the reporting Imam is **al-Kazim**, quoting **al-Sadiq**, addressing one of al-Sadiq's sons.

**Authenticity** — ***da'if*** (weak) — **al-'Allamah Muhammad Baqir al-Majlisi**, *Mir'at al-'Uqul*. **Shaykh Muhammad Baqir al-Behbudi excluded it** from *Sahih al-Kafi* (لم يخرجه). The weak link is Sahl b. Ziyad, whom Ibn al-Ghada'iri and al-Najashi both criticise.

**Confidence** — **STRONGLY VERIFIED** as to text, reference and grading.

**Verdict on the speaker's use** — This is the narration that carries the al-Sadiq attribution he used, and its consequence-clause is **not** what he said. It says the two traits cost you your share of both worlds; it says nothing about seeing things through or about others' rights. And it is graded weak by the standard assessor.

**Practical meaning** — The teaching survives either way and is genuinely useful to a parent: the Imams distinguish **laziness** (won't start / won't do) from ***dajar*** — a compound of impatience, being fed-up, and losing heart partway. The second is the one that ruins *tarbiyah*, because parenting is a long obedience. Note that the al-Kafi setting is a father addressing **his own son** — which is exactly the pastoral use the Shaykh wants. If you want to quote this from the pulpit, say: *"Imam al-Baqir said…"* and cite *Tuhaf al-'Uqul*, noting it is chainless; or quote the al-Kafi wording and name the weakness.

### N2. "Let your son play for seven years" — Imam al-Sadiq

> *Transcript [11:17]:* "that direct command from Imam Sodak where he's like let your son play for seven years."

**ALREADY SETTLED — see `_verified-index.md`.** ***al-Kafi*** 6:47, *Kitab al-'Aqiqa*, *bab ta'dib al-walad*, **h. 3** — «الْغُلَامُ يَلْعَبُ سَبْعَ سِنِينَ…». al-Majlisi: ***muwaththaq***; **Behbudi: *sahih***. This is the soundest of the seven-year narrations and the index recommends it over the variants.

**Confidence** — **STRONGLY VERIFIED.**
**Verdict** — Correct, and correctly attributed. His paraphrase "direct command" is a slight strengthening (h.3 is declarative — "the boy plays seven years" — while it is *h.1* that carries the imperative *da' ibnaka yal'ab*, and that one is ***mursal***), but this is a minor point and he is within bounds.
**⚠ Reminder from the index:** both outcome-bearing seven-year narrations carry an explicit **failure branch** which speakers routinely drop. Guaranteed results are not promised.

### N3. "The child will naturally be *halim* after the seven years"

> *Transcript [13:19]:* "if children are able to … release that energy and we've read this hadith before, the child will naturally be haleem and calm and forbearing after the seven years."

**ALREADY SETTLED — see `_verified-index.md`.** The *halim* payoff **does** exist, but as a **separate narration from Imam al-Kazim**, not from the 7-7-7 chapter:
***al-Kafi*** 6:51, *Kitab al-'Aqiqa*, *bab dhaka' al-ghulam* (ch. 37), **h. 2**; also *Man la yahduruhu al-Faqih* 3:493 — «تُسْتَحَبُّ عَرَامَةُ الصَّبِيِّ فِي صِغَرِهِ لِيَكُونَ حَلِيماً فِي كِبَرِهِ». al-Majlisi: ***majhul***; Behbudi excluded.

**Confidence** — **MODERATELY VERIFIED** (text certain, chain *majhul*).
**Verdict** — The claim is **sourceable**, which is better than the index's earlier finding for the "free play → *halim*" framing suggested. But note three things he elides: (i) it is **al-Kazim**, not al-Sadiq, and not the 7-7-7 hadith he had just quoted; (ii) the operative word is ***'arama*** — boisterousness, unruliness, *rowdiness* — not "releasing energy" in any physiological sense; (iii) the narration is *majhul*. **Also from the index: the corollary he does not state but which circulates — "be worried about a quiet, compliant child" — is NOT in the Arabic.**

### N4. The two companions after Hajj — "Muhammad" and "Murazim"

> *Transcript [28:32–30:34]:* "in the time of Imam Sod[iq] there were two men, one of them's name is Muhammad and the other one's name is Mur[a]zim… Morazim was from Madan, so Iraq. They go over and they're now in Medina. So first they go to Hajj. After Hajj, then they go to Medina. When they go to Medina, Moraz[i]m falls ill… And Muhammad… would just keep going to masjid [al-Nabawi]… so he complained to Muhammad… so Muhammad… went to Imam Sodak… so the imam told him this: he says that **if you sat with him that would have been better than you making salat in the masjid**."

**Arabic** — none can be supplied; no text located.

**Reference** — **NOT LOCATED.** Checked and absent from:
- *al-Kafi* vol. 3, *Kitab al-Jana'iz*, ch. 6 — "How often a patient should be visited, how long the visit should be" (6 hadiths): no Murazim, no Medina, no Prophet's Mosque.
- *al-Kafi* vol. 3, *Kitab al-Jana'iz*, ch. 8 — "The virtue/reward of visiting a patient" (10 hadiths): same result.
- *al-Kafi* vol. 2, *Kitab al-Iman wa'l-Kufr*, ch. 83 — "Assisting a believer in his needs" (14 hadiths): the tawaf-comparison narrations are here, but no Murazim, no sick man, no Prophet's Mosque.

**Chain / Authenticity** — N/A.

**Confidence** — **NEEDS FURTHER VERIFICATION.** ⚠ **Do not attribute this to a hadith collection from the pulpit.**

**Verdict on the speaker's use** — He does not name a source, which is honest as far as it goes, but he presents it as a report from the time of Imam al-Sadiq and puts specific speech in the Imam's mouth. That requires a citation and there is none in evidence.

**What is worth saying in the story's favour** — The two names are not random and the frame is plausible. **Murazim b. Hakim al-Mada'ini** is a genuine, well-known companion of Imams al-Sadiq and al-Kazim, and the *nisba* al-Mada'ini matches the transcript's "from Madan" → **Mada'in** (Ctesiphon, Iraq) exactly. The rijal literature also lists a **Muhammad b. Hakim** alongside him. *(I was unable to re-verify the brotherhood relation in an accessible source in this session — treat it as a lead, not a finding.)* So the story may well be genuine and sitting in *Rijal al-Kashshi*, *Bihar al-Anwar*'s *abwab al-'iyada*, or a Persian *akhlaq* compilation that I could not reach here. **Report it as "a story related about the companions of Imam al-Sadiq" — not as a hadith.**

**Better-sourced substitute — use this instead:**

**Arabic**
> عَلِيُّ بْنُ إِبْرَاهِيمَ عَنْ أَبِيهِ عَنِ ابْنِ أَبِي عُمَيْرٍ عَنِ الْحَكَمِ بْنِ أَيْمَنَ عَنْ أَبَانِ بْنِ تَغْلِبَ قَالَ: سَمِعْتُ أَبَا عَبْدِ اللهِ (عليه السلام) يَقُولُ: مَنْ طَافَ بِالْبَيْتِ أُسْبُوعاً كَتَبَ اللهُ عَزَّ وَجَلَّ لَهُ سِتَّةَ آلَافِ حَسَنَةٍ وَمَحَا عَنْهُ سِتَّةَ آلَافِ سَيِّئَةٍ وَرَفَعَ لَهُ سِتَّةَ آلَافِ دَرَجَةٍ … ثُمَّ قَالَ: **وَقَضَاءُ حَاجَةِ الْمُؤْمِنِ أَفْضَلُ مِنْ طَوَافٍ وَطَوَافٍ** حَتَّى عَدَّ عَشْراً.

**Translation** — "Whoever performs a seven-circuit tawaf of the House, Allah records for him six thousand good deeds, erases six thousand sins and raises him six thousand degrees… Then he said: **and fulfilling the need of a believer is better than a tawaf, and a tawaf** — until he had counted ten."

**Reference** — ***al-Kafi*** vol. 2, *Kitab al-Iman wa'l-Kufr*, *bab qada' hajat al-mu'min* (ch. 83), **h. 6**.
**Chain** — 'Ali b. Ibrahim ← his father ← Ibn Abi 'Umayr ← al-Hakam b. Ayman ← Aban b. Taghlib ← al-Sadiq.
**Authenticity** — ***majhul*** — **al-Majlisi**, *Mir'at al-'Uqul*; **Behbudi excluded it**. (h. 8, via Ishaq b. 'Ammar, carries the same "better than tawaf… ten times" formula.)
**Why it is the better citation** — It makes the identical point (a companion prioritising a ritual in the sanctuary over a brother's need; the Imam correcting the ranking), it is in the primary Shia collection, and it is *majhul* rather than unlocatable — a far stronger footing.

### N5. "Playing is hardwired into their *fitrah*"

> *Transcript [01:00] and [38:43]:* "playing is a real need of our children and it's hardwired into their fitrah" … "The way the child was hardwired, they're supposed to play."

**Reference** — **No narration says this.** The index establishes what the *fitrah* narrations actually gloss *fitrah* as: ***al-tawhid*** and *al-ma'rifa bihi* (*al-Kafi* 2:12–13, *bab fitrat al-khalq 'ala al-tawhid*, h. 1–4; h. 2 and h. 3 ***sahih***). None of them mentions play, and the index already flags that even the *nubuwwah* of the Prophet (s) is absent from that chapter.

**Confidence** — **NEEDS FURTHER VERIFICATION** as a narration; the *underlying* instruction is separately sound.

**Verdict** — This is a **rhetorical use of "fitrah" as a synonym for "innate/hardwired,"** not a citation. It is not false — play *is* on any reading a natural need of children, and *al-Kafi* 6:47 h. 3 (*muwaththaq/sahih*) is a perfectly good warrant for treating it as one. But *fitrah* is a technical term in this tradition with a specific and much narrower gloss in the narrations, and stretching it invites a listener to think a hadith is behind the phrase. **Better formulation:** "the Imams treated play as a need of the child — *al-Kafi* 6:47 h. 3," and leave *fitrah* out of it.

## Attributions to scholars

### S1. Allamah Tabataba'i — the reward of all of *al-Mizan* for one night of nursing a sick mother

> *Transcript [30:34–31:36]:* "This from alamaat [= **Allamah Tabataba'i**, per the index's resolved-names list]. So there was a young man… an only child… his mother had gotten so sick… he just got tired… **I will give you all of the thawab of tafsir al-Mizan if you give me the thawab of one night of you taking care of your sick mother, I'll exchange it with you.**"

**Status — NOT LOCATED.** ⚠ **Do not attribute.** This is one of the most widely circulated stories in contemporary Shia lecture culture (it is also told of Ayatollah Bahjat and of other scholars, which is itself a warning sign of a floating anecdote). No published memoir of Allamah Tabataba'i, no biography, and no primary anecdote collection reachable in this session records it. **Confidence: NEEDS FURTHER VERIFICATION.**

**The two factual asides, checked:**

| Aside | Status |
|---|---|
| *"al-Mizan* took **20 years** to write"| ⚠ **Close but rounded up.** Allamah Tabataba'i produced *al-Mizan* **between 1954 and 1972** — **~18 years**. "Nearly twenty years" would be exact; "20 years" is a mild overstatement. |
| "**How many volumes?**" (asked to the audience, answer not audible) | The standard Arabic edition (Mu'assasat al-A'lami, Beirut) is **20 volumes**. Publication history is genuinely messy — the work is also described as a 27-volume composition, and has been issued in Persian **first in 40 volumes, later condensed to 20**. If a listener answers "20," they are right for the Arabic. |
| — | ⚠ **Worth knowing and not mentioned:** **Allamah Tabataba'i lost his mother when he was five and his father when he was nine.** The story therefore cannot be autobiographical, and any retelling that implies it is should be corrected. |

**Verdict on the speaker's use** — The *moral* is sound and richly supported in the tradition; the *vehicle* is unsourced. Because the vehicle involves a named, revered, recently-living scholar making a specific statement, this is the kind of attribution that most needs a citation and has none.

**Better-sourced substitutes** — Two options, in order of strength:

1. **Sunni, rigorously authentic.** A man came to the Prophet (s) seeking permission to go on jihad. He asked: *"Are your parents alive?"* The man said yes. He said: **«فَفِيهِمَا فَجَاهِدْ»** — *"Then strive in [serving] the two of them."* — **Sahih al-Bukhari 3004**; **Sahih Muslim 2549**. Rigorously authentic, universally accepted, and it makes the speaker's exact point (the more spectacular act of worship is *not* the higher one when a parent needs you).
2. **Imami parallel — but flag the chain.** ***al-Kafi*** vol. 2, *Kitab al-Iman wa'l-Kufr*, *bab al-birr bi'l-walidayn* (ch. 69), **h. 10** and **h. 20**: a young man loves jihad but his parents (h. 20: his mother) dislike his going; the Prophet tells him to stay, *"for her taking comfort in your presence for one night is better than a year of jihad in the way of Allah."* ⚠ **Both run through 'Amr b. Shimr ← Jabir [al-Ju'fi]** — the same weak link the index has already flagged twice elsewhere in this series. Cite it as corroborative, not probative.

### S2. The unnamed Ayatollah and the child prodigy who had memorised the Qur'an

> *Transcript [17:23–18:25]:* "his father comes and sits with Ayat[ollah] … to get some advice … 'I only have one request from you and that is that you have a balanced approach even to this child who's gifted; neither he nor you should have things that just are imposed on him … there shouldn't be a lot of pressure. His tasks should be easy. At the same time, he should be able to go out on trips, have a lot of lawful recreation, changing locations.'"

**Status — NOT LOCATED / UNIDENTIFIABLE.** ⚠ The speaker names no one ("Ayat…", garbled), gives no date, and gives no identifying detail about the child. There is no way to identify or check this. **Confidence: NEEDS FURTHER VERIFICATION.**

**Verdict on the speaker's use** — **This is his weakest attribution in form and one of his soundest in content.** In form: an unnamed marja' giving unrecorded advice about an unnamed child is unfalsifiable and should not be presented as scholarly authority. In content: the advice — don't impose, keep the load light, ensure trips and lawful recreation, vary the environment — is exactly what the developmental literature on gifted children says (see E9 below), and it is *counter*-cultural advice for a religious audience that tends to push hafiz children hard. **He should keep the advice and drop the anonymous *marja'*, or find the citation.**

*Note: several Iranian marja's have documented positions on the pressuring of child huffaz. If the household wants a citable authority here, that is where to look; nothing specific could be pinned in this session.*

### S3. The two-snakes parable

> *Transcript [26:32]:* "**They said not true, inshallah.** They said once there were these two snakes … 'Are we poisonous?' … 'I just bit my tongue. It's going to come back and it bites hard.'"

**Status — FOLK JOKE, not a narration.** This is a widely circulating modern joke (usually: two snakes; "are we venomous?"; "…I just bit my tongue"). It has no basis in the hadith corpus, and nothing about the framing suggests he intends it as one.

**Confidence** — **STRONGLY VERIFIED as folklore** (i.e. it is confidently *not* a narration).

**Verdict on the speaker's use** — **Fine, and to his credit he flagged it.** The caption "They said not true, inshallah" is almost certainly *"They say — not true, inshallah"* or *"there's a story, not true, inshallah,"* i.e. an explicit disclaimer that the story is illustrative. That is exactly the right handling of a parable and is better practice than most of what this series does with anecdotes. No correction needed; a parent should simply understand it as an illustration.

### S4. The large refrigerator / freezer analogy — "some of the scholars"

> *Transcript [27:32]:* "what some of the scholars say is this … imagine yourselves being in a large freezer or not freezer, refrigerator, but it's really cold and everything in this large freezer encourages you to just sit still, but if you sit still you'll freeze to death. You need to move to stay alive."

**Status — NOT LOCATED.** No named scholar; nothing traceable. **Confidence: NEEDS FURTHER VERIFICATION.**

**Verdict on the speaker's use** — Attributing an analogy vaguely to "some of the scholars" is a low-stakes version of the same problem as S2: it borrows authority without supplying it. Since it is an *analogy* rather than a ruling or a report, the cost is small, but he loses nothing by simply saying "here is an image I find useful."

**Is the physiology right?** **Broadly yes, with one caveat worth knowing.** In cold air, voluntary movement and shivering both raise metabolic heat production and are genuinely protective against hypothermia; stillness in cold is dangerous, and this is standard cold-exposure physiology. **The caveat:** in *cold-water immersion* the advice reverses — movement (swimming) increases convective heat loss and shortens survival time, which is why survival guidance teaches the "HELP" (heat escape lessening posture) rather than swimming. So the analogy holds for the setting he describes (a cold room) but is not a universal law of cold. As a metaphor for "a sedentary environment will kill you slowly unless you deliberately move," it works.

### E1. Video games deliver an adrenaline rush greater than physical play, after which physical play "no longer registers"

> *Transcript [12:19–13:19]:* "if a child will play video games there's a certain amount of adrenaline that the child will get … but if they were to play a physical game to get that same drive, that same rush, they wouldn't get it from the regular game … the video games are going to give them such a rush that this child won't later want to play the physical games … Normal games I have to work so hard to get that same amount of a rush." Repeated at [38:43]: "I get such a high off of playing the video game and I don't get that [from physical play]."

**What the research shows**

*The first half is real.* Video games do produce measurable neurochemical reward. The landmark study is **Koepp MJ, Gunn RN, Lawrence AD, Cunningham VJ, Dagher A, Jones T, Brooks DJ, Bench CJ, Grasby PM, "Evidence for striatal dopamine release during a video game," *Nature*, 1998;393(6682):266–268** — [11C]raclopride PET showed dopamine release in the ventral striatum during goal-directed gaming, of a magnitude comparable to intravenous psychostimulants. So "video games give a rush" is not a preacher's exaggeration; it is a genuine finding, and one of the most-cited in the field.

*The second half — that this desensitises the child so that ordinary play "no longer registers" — is not established.* This is a reward-tolerance/anhedonia claim. Evidence for altered reward processing exists mainly in **clinically diagnosed Internet Gaming Disorder samples**, not in ordinary children who play a lot, and even there the direction of causation is unresolved (does gaming blunt reward, or do people with blunted reward game more?). No study demonstrates that recreational gaming raises a child's hedonic threshold such that running and climbing stop being enjoyable.

*What is well established is the weaker, displacement version of his claim — and it is weaker than he implies.* **Marshall SJ, Biddle SJH, Gorely T, Cameron N, Murdey I, "Relationships between media use, body fatness and physical activity in children and youth: a meta-analysis," *International Journal of Obesity*, 2004;28(10):1238–1246** found the association between screen media use and physical activity in children to be **statistically significant but small** — of the order of r ≈ −0.1. Screens and play compete for time, but far less than the "one crowds out the other" picture suggests.

*And the arousal contrast may run the other way.* **Dodd HF, Lester KJ, "Adventurous Play as a Mechanism for Reducing Risk for Childhood Anxiety: A Conceptual Model," *Clinical Child and Family Psychology Review*, 2021;24(1):164–181** describes adventurous physical play as producing precisely "feelings of fear and excitement, thrill and adrenaline" — i.e. real physical play is itself a potent arousal experience, which undercuts the premise that it is the pallid option.

**Fair verdict — PARTLY SUPPORTED, OVERSTATED AT THE KEY STEP.**
He is right that gaming is neurochemically rewarding, and right that gaming and play compete. He is not entitled to the strong claim that gaming *ruins the child's capacity to enjoy physical play* — that is a mechanism he asserts without evidence, and it is the step his whole argument turns on. **Recommended reformulation for a parent:** "Screens are engineered to be more immediately rewarding than most things, and they take time away from play. Play is also thrilling, but it takes longer to get going." That is defensible; "the child won't later want to play" is not.

### E2. "Non-renewable energy" of the first seven years

> *Transcript [13:19]:* "that natural energy that Allah placed in those children, that natural energy needs to be expelled. **We called it non-renewable energy** when we talked about it before. In the first seven years they have to run and jump and bounce off the walls in order to get that non-renewable energy out." And [14:20]: unreleased energy is "stored inside them" and "comes back to bite" the parent.

**What the research shows**

**"Non-renewable energy" is not a term in developmental psychology, exercise physiology, paediatrics or the hadith corpus.** It is the speaker's own coinage (he says so — "we called it"). Taken literally as an energy-storage claim ("that energy wasn't released… now that new energy is stored inside them") it is **physiologically false**: children do not accumulate an undischarged reservoir of kinetic energy. The nearest real phenomenon is behavioural, not energetic — under-exercised children show more restlessness and poorer self-regulation *at the time*, not a growing stockpile.

**But the intuition it gestures at is real, and there is a proper name for it.** Two genuine bodies of work:

1. **Sensitive periods for motor development.** **Stodden DF, Goodway JD, Langendorfer SJ, Roberton MA, Rudisill ME, Garcia C, Garcia LE, "A Developmental Perspective on the Role of Motor Skill Competence in Physical Activity: An Emergent Relationship," *Quest*, 2008;60(2):290–306** sets out the "proficiency barrier": fundamental movement skills built in early childhood determine whether a child can later participate in physical activity at all, and a child who misses that window enters a self-reinforcing spiral of low competence → low participation → lower competence. This is the *real* version of "you can't get those years back," and it is a strong, well-replicated framework.
2. **Sensitive periods generally** — the amblyopia case he himself raises (E8) is the textbook example.

**Fair verdict — RIGHT INSTINCT, WRONG MECHANISM, INVENTED TERM.**
The claim "there is something time-limited about early childhood movement that you cannot make up later" is **defensible and important**. The claim that energy is a finite quantity that gets stored if not discharged is **not**. He should say "proficiency barrier" or simply "a window that closes," and drop the energy-accounting language — which is also what makes his "stored inside them" explanation of later misbehaviour unsupportable.

### E3. Claimed outcomes of heavy screen use: moodiness, irritability, lack of grit, need for instant gratification, unnatural stubbornness, mischief, nervous tics

> *Transcript [14:20–15:20]:* "some of these kids end up being very moody, very irritable… some of them end up not having grit. They can't see something through. they just need to always have this kind of instant gratification. Some of those kids are going to end up being very unnaturally stubborn… some of these children become unnaturally mischievous… And then sometimes there's even physical effects. **You might see like a nervous tick** right from the kid who's doing that."

**This is the most contested literature in child development. The honest answer is to report the contest, not pick a side.**

**(a) Moodiness / irritability / behaviour problems — REAL BUT SMALL, AND BIDIRECTIONAL.**
- **Eirich R, McArthur BA, Anhorn C, McGuinness C, Christakis DA, Madigan S, "Association of Screen Time With Internalizing and Externalizing Behavior Problems in Children 12 Years or Younger: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis," *JAMA Psychiatry*, 2022;79(5):393–405** — the definitive synthesis. Screen time is associated with both externalizing (acting out, irritability, aggression) and internalizing problems, but the **correlations are small** (on the order of r ≈ .10 and below), and the review found evidence that the relationship runs **in both directions**: difficult behaviour predicts more screen time about as well as screen time predicts difficult behaviour. A child who is already dysregulated gets handed a tablet.
- **Twenge JM, Campbell WK, "Associations between screen time and lower psychological well-being among children and adolescents: Evidence from a population-based study," *Preventive Medicine Reports*, 2018;12:271–283** — in a large US sample, high screen users (7+ h/day) were roughly twice as likely as low users (1 h/day) to have been diagnosed with anxiety or depression, and showed lower emotional stability, more argumentativeness and more difficulty with friendships. **Cross-sectional**, and Twenge is on the alarmed end of this debate.
- Against: **Orben A, Przybylski AK, "The association between adolescent well-being and digital technology use," *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2019;3(2):173–182** — specification-curve analysis across three large datasets (>350,000 adolescents) found digital technology use explains **about 0.4% of the variance** in wellbeing, an association the authors note is comparable in size to eating potatoes or wearing glasses. This paper is the most influential sceptical counterweight in the field and no fair presentation should omit it.

**(b) "Lack of grit" — UNSUPPORTED, AND THE CONSTRUCT ITSELF IS SHAKY.**
No study links screen use to reduced grit. Moreover, **Credé M, Tynan MC, Harms PD, "Much ado about grit: A meta-analytic synthesis of the grit literature," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2017;113(3):492–511** found grit to be largely redundant with the established trait of conscientiousness and only weakly predictive of performance. Building a parenting warning on "grit" is building on sand.

**(c) "Need for instant gratification" — UNSUPPORTED, AND THE UNDERLYING CLASSIC IS WEAKER THAN BELIEVED.**
There is no good evidence that screens cause reduced delay of gratification in children. And **Watts TW, Duncan GJ, Quan H, "Revisiting the Marshmallow Test: A Conceptual Replication Investigating Links Between Early Delay of Gratification and Later Outcomes," *Psychological Science*, 2018;29(7):1159–1177** — a pre-registered conceptual replication in a much larger and more diverse sample — found the famous predictive power of delay of gratification largely **disappears once family background and early cognitive ability are controlled**. The popular "marshmallow test" story that underwrites this whole line of parenting rhetoric does not survive replication intact.

**(d) "Unnaturally stubborn" / "unnaturally mischievous" — NOT MEASURABLE CLAIMS.**
These are not constructs anyone studies. To the extent they map onto oppositional behaviour, see (a): small, bidirectional associations.

**(e) NERVOUS TICS — ⭐ HIS STRONGEST SCREEN CLAIM, AND HE APPEARS NOT TO KNOW IT.**
This is the one item on his list with a striking and specific evidence base. From late 2020 through 2022, paediatric neurology clinics across Europe, North America and Australia recorded an abrupt international surge in adolescents (overwhelmingly girls) presenting with sudden-onset, explosive tic-like movements and vocalisations — an atypical presentation that did not fit Tourette syndrome. Multiple independent groups traced the phenomenon to **exposure to tic-portraying content on social media**, especially TikTok. The key paper is **Müller-Vahl KR, Pisarenko A, Jakubovski E, Fremer C, "Stop that! It's not Tourette's but a new type of mass sociogenic illness," *Brain*, 2022;145(2):476–480**, which characterises it as a **mass sociogenic illness spread through a social-media "virtual" index case**. Parallel reports appeared in *Movement Disorders* and the *Journal of the American Academy of Child & Adolescent Psychiatry* over the same period.

**So: screen exposure demonstrably produced tic-like behaviour in children, at scale, in the last few years.** Three qualifications the Shaykh's framing gets wrong, though:
- It is **social media (video content of other people ticcing)**, not video games.
- It is **adolescents**, overwhelmingly **girls aged roughly 12–19** — not the under-sevens who are this lecture's subject.
- These are **functional tic-like behaviours**, not Tourette syndrome. There is no evidence that screens cause Tourette's or any primary tic disorder.

**Fair verdict on E3 overall — MIXED, AND HE OVERSELLS THE MIDDLE OF HIS LIST WHILE UNDERSELLING THE END.**
Moodiness/irritability: real, small, and probably partly the reverse of what he thinks (irritable children get more screens). Grit and instant gratification: assertions, and both rest on constructs that have taken heavy replication damage. Stubbornness and mischief: unmeasurable. Nervous tics: **the one item where reality is more alarming than his throwaway line**, though for a different population and a different mechanism. **A parent should treat his list as a set of worries to watch for, not a set of established consequences** — and should know that the single largest and most careful analysis in the field (Orben & Przybylski 2019) finds the overall screen-wellbeing effect to be very small.

### E4. Active play reduces susceptibility to illness / improves immune function

> *Transcript [10:17]:* "Those kids who are physically active… they're going to be **less susceptible to getting sick** than those kids who always stay… **It's going to help their immune system.**"

**What the research shows — ⭐ THIS IS HIS BEST-SUPPORTED CLAIM, AND IT IS BETTER SUPPORTED THAN HE ARGUED FOR IT.**

1. **Exercise and infection risk.** **Nieman DC, Wentz LM, "The compelling link between physical activity and the body's defense system," *Journal of Sport and Health Science*, 2019;8(3):201–217** — the standard review. Regular **moderate** physical activity is associated with reduced incidence and severity of upper respiratory tract infection, enhanced immunosurveillance (acute bouts mobilise NK cells, T cells and immunoglobulins), and lower systemic inflammation. ⚠ Note the shape of the curve: the benefit is for *moderate* activity; the older "open window" claim that heavy prolonged exertion suppresses immunity is now contested rather than settled.
2. **Outdoor play specifically, in young children, experimentally.** **Roslund MI, Puhakka R, Grönroos M, Nurminen N, Oikarinen S, Gazali AM, Cinek O, Kramná L, Siter N, Vari HK, Soininen L, Parajuli A, Rajaniemi J, Kinnunen T, Laitinen OH, Hyöty H, Sinkkonen A, and the ADELE research group, "Biodiversity intervention enhances immune regulation and health-associated commensal microbiota among daycare children," *Science Advances*, 2020;6(42):eaba2578.** Finnish daycares had their paved yards replaced with forest floor, sod and planters; children played on them for 28 days. Result: measurable shifts in skin and gut microbiota toward health-associated commensals, and changes in immunoregulatory markers (raised plasma TGF-β1 and altered cytokine profile) relative to control daycares. **This is an intervention study in the exact population the lecture is about, and it supports him.**
3. Supporting mechanism: outdoor time raises vitamin D status, itself linked to respiratory infection risk.

**Fair verdict — SUPPORTED. He is on firmer ground here than anywhere else in the lecture, and he passed over it in a single sentence.** The only softening needed: this is about *regular moderate activity and outdoor exposure*, not about play as such, and the effect sizes are modest — a physically active child still catches colds. **If he wants one citation to give parents, Roslund 2020 is it.**

### E5. Active play develops intelligence and memory

> *Transcript [09:15]:* "It'll teach a child to use their intelligence and their memory. When you watch these children go through a game, they go through it one time, two times, after a while, they're watching, they're learning… **It's an indirect method of teaching.**"

**What the research shows**

**For physical activity → executive function and memory: well supported, with real experimental evidence.**
- **Hillman CH, Pontifex MB, Castelli DM, Khan NA, Raine LB, Scudder MR, Drollette ES, Moore RD, Wu C-T, Kamijo K, "Effects of the FITKids Randomized Controlled Trial on Executive Control and Brain Function," *Pediatrics*, 2014;134(4):e1063–e1071** — a 9-month after-school physical activity **randomised controlled trial** in 221 prepubertal children. The intervention group improved on inhibition and cognitive flexibility, with corresponding changes in event-related brain potentials. **This is an RCT, not a correlation.**
- Related work from the same group links aerobic fitness in children to larger hippocampal volume and better relational memory — directly relevant to his "memory" claim.

**For pretend/free play → cognitive development: much weaker than commonly claimed, and he should know this.**
**Lillard AS, Lerner MD, Hopkins EJ, Dore RA, Smith ED, Palmquist CM, "The impact of pretend play on children's development: A review of the evidence," *Psychological Bulletin*, 2013;139(1):1–34** reviewed the literature on pretend play and found the evidence for a *causal* role in creativity, intelligence, problem-solving, social skills, language and executive function to be **weak** — most studies suffer from experimenter bias and inadequate controls. The authors' conclusion is that pretend play is at most one of several routes to these outcomes, and possibly epiphenomenal.

**Fair verdict — SUPPORTED FOR THE PHYSICAL HALF, OVERSTATED FOR THE "LEARNING THROUGH PLAY" HALF.**
Because this lecture is specifically about **active, physical** play, he lands on the better-supported side of the divide almost by accident. His stronger framing — that games are "an indirect method of teaching" through which children "pick up many things" — is exactly the claim Lillard et al. found the field cannot substantiate. **Keep: physical activity improves executive control and memory (Hillman 2014, RCT). Drop or soften: play teaches content indirectly.**

### E6. Active play "gets rid of the anxiety in children" and makes them brave

> *Transcript [08:15–09:15]:* "our children turn out brave. They can scrape their knee, they can lose, they can fall down… **It's going to help get rid of the anxiety in children** if they can play these active games. Our children will learn independence…"

**What the research shows**

- **Dodd HF, Lester KJ, "Adventurous Play as a Mechanism for Reducing Risk for Childhood Anxiety: A Conceptual Model," *Clinical Child and Family Psychology Review*, 2021;24(1):164–181** — proposes and marshals evidence that adventurous play (climbing, height, speed, rough-and-tumble) works as a natural form of **interoceptive and situational exposure**: the child repeatedly experiences fear-linked arousal in a context they control and can master, which builds tolerance of uncertainty and of physiological arousal — the two things anxiety disorders are built on. **This is precisely the Shaykh's "scrape their knee, fall down, turn out brave" intuition, formalised.** Dodd's associated survey work finds children who report more adventurous play show fewer internalising symptoms.
- **Brussoni M et al., "What is the relationship between risky outdoor play and health in children? A systematic review," *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*, 2015;12(6):6423–6454** — systematic review concluding that risky outdoor play is associated with better physical and social health outcomes and that the injury risks are commonly overestimated relative to the developmental benefits.
- ⚠ **But "gets rid of" is far too strong.** Dodd & Lester 2021 is explicitly a **conceptual model**, and the supporting evidence is largely cross-sectional. Exercise interventions for child and adolescent anxiety produce **small to moderate** reductions in symptoms, not elimination. Childhood anxiety is substantially heritable and multiply determined; no amount of play "eliminates" it, and a parent whose anxious child plays outside daily should not conclude that the anxiety is therefore their own failure.

**Fair verdict — MECHANISM CORRECT, MAGNITUDE OVERSTATED.**
This is a genuinely good insight, better supported than the audience would guess, and the "brave" half is squarely backed by the risky-play literature. But **"eliminates anxiety" is a claim no study supports and one that could do real harm** if a parent of an anxious child hears it as a diagnosis of their parenting. **Reformulate as: "adventurous play is one of the few things known to lower a child's risk of developing anxiety."** That is both accurate and rhetorically strong enough.

### E7. Screens as the easy substitute; the modern lifestyle removes movement

> *Transcript [22:29–24:30]:* buses, pay phones, maps before GPS, DoorDash, curbside pickup, circling the Target car park for a closer space; "children are the opponents to the modern lifestyle."

**Status — SUPPORTED, and uncontroversial.** The decline in children's independent mobility and active travel to school across high-income countries over the last four decades is one of the better-documented trends in public health; so is the association between built environment/car dependence and reduced physical activity. His observational, generational framing here is sound and needs no correction.

⚠ One nuance he does not draw and should: **the strongest and cleanest screen-related finding in the whole child literature is about sleep**, not behaviour — screen use displaces and delays sleep, and sleep loss then drives irritability, poorer attention and worse self-regulation. ### E8. The lazy eye (amblyopia) analogy

> *Transcript [25:32]:* "In Iran, sometimes there were a lot of people who would suffer from what we call the lazy eye… Now if that problem was addressed early and the person forced them let's say to wear a patch then the other eye would get strong. Nobody did that, now the person is living with that for the rest of their lives."

**What the research shows — the analogy is well chosen and broadly correct; three specifics need adjusting.**

| His claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Amblyopia is a real condition, common, and treated by patching | ✅ **Correct.** Amblyopia affects roughly 2–3% of children. The US National Eye Institute: treatment is patching the stronger eye (from ~2 hours a day up to all waking hours), atropine drops to blur the strong eye as an alternative, plus corrective lenses. |
| **Which eye gets patched** — "if the person forced them to wear a patch then **the other eye would get strong**" | ⚠ **Correct in substance, easy to hear backwards.** You patch the **strong** eye to force the **weak** eye to work. His sentence, read carefully, says this ("the other eye would get strong" = the eye that isn't patched). But said quickly from a pulpit it can be heard as patching the weak eye, which would be exactly wrong. Worth stating explicitly. |
| Treat early or the person "is living with that for the rest of their lives" | ⚠ **Directionally right, too absolute.** NEI: *"It's important to start treating children with amblyopia early — the sooner the better"* and *"Amblyopia treatment is usually less effective in adults than in children,"* with *"kids who grow up without treatment may have lifelong vision problems."* **But the field revised the hard cut-off.** The Pediatric Eye Disease Investigator Group's *"Randomized Trial of Treatment of Amblyopia in Children Aged 7 to 17 Years," Archives of Ophthalmology, 2005;123(4):437–447* found that a substantial proportion of children **aged 7 to 17** still responded to treatment — most strongly among those **not previously treated**. So the window narrows sharply; it does not slam shut at seven. |

**Fair verdict — HIS BEST ANALOGY IN THE LECTURE.**
Amblyopia is the textbook clinical example of a **sensitive period** — exactly the concept he needs for E2 and cannot otherwise name — and unlike "non-renewable energy" it is real, measurable and familiar. It also carries the right moral: the intervention is unpleasant for the child, the parent must impose it, and the cost of not doing so is permanent. **Recommendation to the household: keep this analogy, add "you patch the good eye," and soften "rest of their lives" to "much harder to fix later."**

### E10. Wide sibling age gaps, or an only child, increase the play burden on the parent

> *Transcript [36:42–37:42]:* "Some of us have too few children… if you have only one child… that's a lot of weight to be on one person. If I have several children… one of the best playmates for kids are other kids… having too few children — by the way, even if there's a huge age gap between our children, then our children won't be able to get the next kid… if we have children close in age it'll be easier for them to engage."

**What the research shows — SUPPORTED, and more solidly than the surrounding material.**

1. **Only children do receive more parental time per child.** **Price J, "Parent-Child Quality Time: Does Birth Order Matter?" *Journal of Human Resources*, 2008;43(1):240–265** — using American time-use diaries, first-born and only children receive substantially more one-on-one parental time at a given age than later-borns do; each additional child dilutes per-child parental time. The mechanical corollary is exactly the Shaykh's point: **with one child, the parent is the play partner, because there is no one else.**
2. **Siblings close in age genuinely function as play partners; wide gaps reduce joint play.** The sibling-relationship literature (Judith Dunn's foundational work from the early 1980s onward) established that closely-spaced siblings engage in far more joint pretend and physical play, while wide age gaps produce a more asymmetric, caregiving-shaped relationship rather than a peer-play one. Developmental-stage mismatch is the mechanism, which is precisely how he puts it ("I'm in a different stage of my life and now this kid is…").
3. He also anticipates and rejects the counter-argument ("some people think the opposite — if I only have one kid then I'll be able to give them all this time"). Price 2008 shows both are true at once: the only child gets more parental time **and** that is more demanding on the parent, because it is not shared with a sibling.
4. **Critically, he does not draw the wrong conclusion.** He explicitly says having other children "doesn't absolve me of my need to play with them" — "playing with children is great, but playing with your parents is something different." That is the right qualification and it is supported: sibling play and parent-child play are not interchangeable developmentally.

⚠ **One caution from the shared index:** the index already records that on *health* outcomes, a *Frontiers in Pediatrics* 2022 systematic review (7 studies, >65,500 children) found **no optimal birth interval established** in high-income settings. That finding is about health, not play companionship, and does not contradict him here — but it does mean that "close spacing is better" should not be generalised into a health or fiqh recommendation.

**Fair verdict — SUPPORTED, WELL-QUALIFIED, AND HE STAYED INSIDE HIS EVIDENCE.** One of the better-argued passages in the lecture. The only thing to add is that this is a description of a *logistical* reality, not a prescription about family size, and he does not quite say so.

## Better-sourced substitutes

For a parent who wants to teach this lecture's content from citable material:

1. **For "the Imams told us to let children play"** → ***al-Kafi*** 6:47, *Kitab al-'Aqiqa*, *bab ta'dib al-walad*, **h.3** — «الْغُلَامُ يَلْعَبُ سَبْعَ سِنِينَ، وَيَتَعَلَّمُ الْكِتَابَ سَبْعَ سِنِينَ، وَيَتَعَلَّمُ الْحَلَالَ وَالْحَرَامَ سَبْعَ سِنِينَ». **al-Majlisi: *muwaththaq*; Behbudi: *sahih*.** The single soundest text in this whole territory. Quote the failure branch too.

2. **For "a boisterous child becomes a forbearing adult"** → ***al-Kafi*** 6:51, ch.37, **h.2**, from **Imam al-Kazim** — «تُسْتَحَبُّ عَرَامَةُ الصَّبِيِّ فِي صِغَرِهِ لِيَكُونَ حَلِيماً فِي كِبَرِهِ». *Majhul*, so present it as encouragement, not proof — and attribute it to **al-Kazim**, separately from the seven-year hadith.

3. **For "beware of the two traits that wreck a parent"** → ***Tuhaf al-'Uqul***, short maxims of **Imam al-Baqir**, **no. 31** — «إِيَّاكَ وَالْكَسَلَ وَالضَّجَرَ، فَإِنَّهُمَا مِفْتَاحُ كُلِّ شَرٍّ؛ مَنْ كَسِلَ لَمْ يُؤَدِّ حَقّاً، وَمَنْ ضَجِرَ لَمْ يَصْبِرْ عَلَى حَقٍّ». Chainless — say so. This is the version whose meaning the Shaykh gave.

4. **For "a brother's need outranks the ritual you came for"** (replacing the Murazim story) → ***al-Kafi*** 2, *bab qada' hajat al-mu'min* (ch.83), **h.6**, Aban b. Taghlib ← al-Sadiq: «وَقَضَاءُ حَاجَةِ الْمُؤْمِنِ أَفْضَلُ مِنْ طَوَافٍ وَطَوَافٍ» — *"and fulfilling a believer's need is better than a tawaf, and a tawaf"* — until he had counted ten. **al-Majlisi: *majhul*.** Same lesson, in the primary collection, in the Haram.

5. **For "caring for a sick parent outranks the greater deed"** (replacing the *al-Mizan* story) → **Sahih al-Bukhari 3004 / Sahih Muslim 2549**: the man who sought permission for jihad and was told «فَفِيهِمَا فَجَاهِدْ» — *"then strive in [serving] the two of them."* **Rigorously authentic.** Imami parallel with the same meaning: ***al-Kafi*** 2, *bab al-birr bi'l-walidayn* (ch.69), **h.10 and h.20** — ⚠ **both through 'Amr b. Shimr ← Jabir al-Ju'fi**, the weak link this series keeps landing on. Use the Sunni text for strength, the Imami text for resonance, and name both statuses.

6. **For "act early, the window closes"** (replacing "non-renewable energy") → keep the **amblyopia** analogy, which is real; and if a scientific term is wanted, use the **proficiency barrier** (Stodden et al., *Quest* 2008).

7. **For "screens do measurable harm"** (replacing the unsupported grit/gratification claims) → use the two claims that hold: **sleep displacement**, and **outdoor play's demonstrated effect on immune regulation** (Roslund et al., *Science Advances* 2020). Both are concrete, both are experimental, and neither requires overstating.
